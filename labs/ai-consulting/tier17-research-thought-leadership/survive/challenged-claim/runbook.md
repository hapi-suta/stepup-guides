# SURVIVE Runbook: A Published Claim Is Challenged

**Tier 17 - SURVIVE scenario 1 of 3.** Review-assessed. No script to run; a reviewer grades your response against the checklist.

## The situation

You published a technical article making a claim - say, "per-language evaluation reveals quality gaps that blended metrics hide, typically 15 to 25 points." A respected practitioner publicly pushes back: **"That range is overstated, your evidence is anecdotal, and you are generalizing from too few deployments."** Their comment is getting traction. Your credibility is on the line, in public. How you respond in the next few hours matters more than the original article.

This scenario tests whether you can defend a claim with evidence or correct it with grace - and never dig in on something you cannot support.

---

## Diagnosis: are you right, partly right, or wrong?

Do not respond emotionally and do not respond yet. First find out where you actually stand.

### 1. Restate exactly what you claimed
Go back to the published text. Challenges frequently attack a stronger claim than you actually made. Did you say "typically 15 to 25 points" (a hedge) or "always at least 15 points" (absolute)? Precision here decides everything. If you were appropriately hedged, part of the challenge may already be answered.

### 2. Re-examine your evidence
Pull your actual data. How many deployments? How were they measured? Is the range you published supported by what you have, or did you round optimistically? Be brutally honest with yourself. The critic may be right that three deployments cannot support a universal range.

### 3. Separate the valid part of the challenge from the invalid part
Most good challenges are partly right. Perhaps your *direction* is solid (low-resource languages do lag) but your *magnitude* claim was too strong for your sample. Identify precisely which part you can defend and which part you cannot.

---

## Recovery

### If you can fully defend the claim
1. Respond calmly and publicly. Thank them for engaging - it signals confidence.
2. Restate the claim precisely, including any hedges the critic missed.
3. Produce the evidence: your data, method, and any supporting sources.
4. Acknowledge the reasonable part of their concern ("you are right that a larger sample would strengthen this"), then show why the claim still holds.
5. Do not win by volume or tone. Win by evidence.

### If the claim was partly wrong (the common case)
1. Concede the specific part that was overstated, clearly and quickly.
2. Defend the part that stands, with evidence.
3. Update the article with a visible edit note: "Updated: the 15-25 point range reflects our three measured deployments and should be read as directional, not universal."
4. Thank the critic. You just made your work more defensible.

### If the claim was wrong
1. Issue a prompt, clear, public correction. Say what was wrong and what is right.
2. Thank the person who caught it, by name.
3. Do not bury it or quietly delete the article - that costs more credibility than the error.
4. Log it in your challenges log. A well-handled correction is proof of integrity.

### What you must never do
- Argue defensively about something you cannot support.
- Attack the critic's tone or motives instead of the substance.
- Silently edit the claim to match the criticism and pretend it always said that.
- Fabricate additional evidence to prop up a shaky claim.

---

## The lesson

Publishing makes you challengeable, and that is the point. Credibility comes from claims that survive challenge, not claims that were never tested. The discipline is upstream: the pre-publish check ("can I produce evidence if challenged?") is what makes this scenario survivable. If you published only what you could defend and hedged honestly, a challenge strengthens you. If you overclaimed, a gracious correction still saves you. Digging in is the only move that destroys you.

---

## Review checklist (reviewer grades against this)

- [ ] Re-read the exact published claim before responding
- [ ] Re-examined the actual evidence honestly, not defensively
- [ ] Separated the valid part of the challenge from the invalid part
- [ ] Chose the correct path: defend with evidence, partial correction, or full correction
- [ ] Responded calmly and publicly, thanking the challenger
- [ ] Produced real evidence when defending (no fabrication)
- [ ] Made any correction visible rather than burying or deleting
- [ ] Logged the challenge and outcome
- [ ] Did not attack the critic or dig in on the unsupportable
