# SURVIVE Runbook: A Talk Goes Wrong Live

**Tier 17 - SURVIVE scenario 3 of 3.** Review-assessed. No script to run; a reviewer grades your response and their observation of your recovery against the checklist.

## The situation

You are on stage at a conference, delivering your flagship talk to a room of practitioners and a few potential clients. You reach the live demo of your assistant. You type the query. Nothing happens. The connection has dropped, or the model is timing out, or the laptop just froze. The room is watching you. This is the moment that separates people who know their material from people who were relying on the demo to carry them.

This scenario tests whether you can recover in front of an audience without losing the room.

---

## Diagnosis: what failed, and does it matter to the talk?

You have about ten seconds, so diagnosis is fast and mental, not thorough.

### 1. Is it recoverable in a few seconds?
Glance: is it a dropped wifi you can reconnect, or is it dead? Do not spend more than a few seconds troubleshooting live. The audience's patience for silent fiddling is very short.

### 2. Does the talk need the demo?
If you prepared correctly, the answer is no. The demo illustrates the argument; it is not the argument. This is why the preparation rule is "the talk must work without the demo at all."

---

## Recovery (the calm sequence)

1. **Acknowledge once, briefly, without spiraling.** "The live version is having a network moment - that is the internet's contribution to my talk." One light line, then move. Do not apologize repeatedly; the audience mirrors your composure.

2. **Go to your backup.** Play the recorded demo you prepared and narrate it as if live: "Here is that same query running - watch what happens with the citations." A recording is nearly as convincing as live, and the audience barely notices the switch when you are calm.

3. **If there is no recording, describe it from mastery.** "Let me walk you through what you would have seen." Use a slide, a whiteboard, or just your words. If you know your system deeply, you can narrate the exact behavior. This actually impresses people because it proves you did not need the crutch.

4. **Return to the argument.** The demo was one illustration. Get back to your claim, your evidence, and your takeaway - the parts that do not depend on a network.

5. **Do not fix it live for long.** If it comes back on its own, great, show it later. Do not hold the room hostage while you debug.

---

## Prevention (this is where the scenario is really won)

You survive a live demo failure by preparing before the talk, not by improvising during it:

- **Record a backup video** of the demo working, and have it one click away.
- **Build the talk to stand without the demo.** The demo is seasoning, not the meal.
- **Rehearse the failure.** Actually practice the sentence you will say and the switch to the backup.
- **Test everything on the venue's network** before you go on, and have an offline fallback.
- **Never put a claim on a slide that only the live demo can support.**

A talk that recovers gracefully from a failed demo often earns more respect than one where everything worked, because it shows the speaker knows the material cold.

---

## The lesson

The demo will fail eventually - it is not a question of if. Amateurs stake the whole talk on it and panic; professionals treat it as illustration and have a backup. Composure is a skill you prepare, not a trait you hope for. The room forgives a technical failure instantly if you stay calm; it never forgives a speaker who falls apart.
