# Concepts: Research Themes for AI-Consulting Thought Leadership

**Tier 17, Module 17.1** - the research agenda that turns you from a practitioner into a recognized voice.

This is a teaching reference. Thought leadership is not writing opinions; it is making a defensible contribution to a body of knowledge and defending it in public. A theme is worth pursuing only if it is (a) something you can speak to from real work, (b) underserved by existing writing, and (c) valuable to the people you want as clients. This module gives you the recommended themes, how to evaluate a theme, and how to turn a theme into publishable work.

Why this matters for a consultant: your writing is your highest-leverage marketing and your credibility moat. A single strong, honest article that a client's leadership actually reads does more than a hundred cold emails. But weak or unsupported writing destroys credibility faster than silence.

---

## What counts as a research contribution (versus an opinion)

An opinion says "I think AI governance matters." A contribution says "here is a repeatable method for AI governance in developing-economy public institutions, here is the evidence it works, and here is where it does not." The difference is:

- **A claim** that is specific and falsifiable.
- **Evidence** from real work, data, or careful analysis.
- **A method** others can reuse.
- **Honest limits** - where it does not apply.

If you cannot state your claim, your evidence, and your limits in three sentences, it is not yet a contribution.

## The recommended research themes

These are chosen because they sit at the intersection of your real work, an underserved market, and genuine need. Pick one or two to go deep on; the rest can be article-sized.

### Responsible AI adoption in African public institutions
The flagship theme. Most AI governance writing assumes rich, high-capacity Western agencies. African public institutions face different constraints: infrastructure, connectivity, skills, budgets, multilingual populations, and data-sovereignty concerns. A method that actually works under these constraints is a real contribution and is close to SUTA Labs' mission.

### Secure AI assistants for government
How to build citizen and staff assistants that are safe, accountable, and appealable. Combines your security (Tier 11) and public-sector (Tier 16) work. Underserved because most assistant writing ignores the accountability and security bar.

### AI governance for developing economies
Adapting frameworks like the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, see: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) and ISO/IEC 42001:2023, the first international AI management system standard (see: https://www.iso.org/standard/42001), to lower-resource, lower-capacity contexts without watering down the protection. The gap: existing frameworks assume resources that many organizations do not have.

### Multilingual RAG for English/French/Pidgin
A concrete technical contribution. Most RAG writing is English-only. Real African and multilingual deployments must retrieve and answer well across languages with uneven training data. Genuine, demonstrable, and rare.

### AI security for public-sector systems
Applying the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (part of the OWASP GenAI Security Project; the current edition is 2025, led by Prompt Injection - see: https://genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10/) and threat modeling specifically to government deployments, where the attack surface meets legal accountability. High value, low competition.

### AI adoption readiness in African organizations
A readiness model and data on what actually blocks adoption on the ground - not the generic maturity models, but evidence from real assessments.

### AI infrastructure sovereignty
Where models run, where data lives, and what it means to depend on foreign providers for public services. A rising policy concern with little practical writing behind it.

### Human oversight of government AI
How to design oversight that is genuine rather than a rubber-stamp, and how to prove it held (this connects directly to the Tier 16 appeals scenario). A design-and-evidence contribution.

### LLMOps for regulated environments
Operating LLM applications where you must monitor behavior, prove auditability, and survive examination. Extends your Tier 10 and Tier 16 work into an operating discipline others have not documented well for regulated contexts.

## How to evaluate a theme before you invest in it

Score each candidate 1-5 on three axes:

| Axis | Question |
|---|---|
| Authority | Can I speak to this from real work, not just reading? |
| Gap | Is this underserved by existing high-quality writing? |
| Value | Do my target clients care about this? |

A theme scoring high on all three is worth a deep, multi-piece investment. A theme high on gap but low on authority is a research project, not an article yet - do the work first. A theme high on authority but low on gap is already crowded; find your specific angle or move on.

## From theme to publishable pieces (the ladder)

A single strong theme produces a whole ladder of artifacts, from quick to deep:

1. **Short article** - one idea, well argued (a day or two of work).
2. **Explainer** - a governance or technical concept made clear for a specific audience.
3. **Case study** - a real engagement, anonymized, with method and results.
4. **Policy brief** - a decision-ready summary for leadership or policymakers.
5. **Conference talk** - the theme presented and defended live.
6. **Open-source tool or template** - the method, made reusable by others.
7. **Peer-reviewed or doctoral publication** - the theme held to external scrutiny.

You climb the ladder over months. Each rung reuses the one below. The Tier 17 publication target is essentially "produce a full ladder for your flagship theme, plus breadth across the others."

## The ethics of thought leadership (non-negotiable)

- **No hallucinated facts or citations.** Every claim is grounded in a real source or your own documented work. A fabricated citation, if caught, ends your credibility.
- **Anonymize client work** properly. Get permission, remove identifying detail, and never expose a client's weaknesses in a way that harms them.
- **State your limits.** Overclaiming is the fastest way to be discredited on review.
- **Correct openly.** If you are wrong, issue a correction. It builds more trust than never being wrong.

## One-line glossary

| Term | One line |
|---|---|
| Research contribution | A specific, evidenced, reusable claim with honest limits - not an opinion. |
| Theme | A durable subject area you build a body of work around. |
| The ladder | Article -> explainer -> case study -> policy brief -> talk -> tool -> peer-reviewed. |
| Authority / Gap / Value | The three axes for judging whether a theme is worth your investment. |
| Anonymization | Removing identifying detail so client work can be published safely. |
