# INTERVIEW: Tier 15 - AI Consulting Mastery

**Tier 15 - Interview preparation**

These are the questions a client executive, a prospective employer, or a partner might ask to test whether you can run an AI engagement end to end - discovery, readiness, deliverables, executive communication, change management, and the commercial side. This tier is where you are hired as a consultant, so the questions test judgment, communication, and commercial discipline together.

For each question you get:
- **The question** as it might be asked.
- **A strong answer** you can adapt in your own words.
- **How to answer** - the technique that makes it land.
- **Why they ask this** - the real thing being tested.

Practice out loud. Aim for a clear 60-90 second answer.

---

## Q1. How do you run an AI readiness assessment?

**Strong answer:**
"I run it in three phases: discover, assess, recommend. Discovery is interviews with executives and department heads, workflow observation, document review, and a technical and data assessment - I need the real current state, not the org chart's version. Then I score readiness across a defined set of dimensions - strategy, leadership, data, technology, security, governance, talent, culture, process maturity, budget, compliance, and vendor readiness - each on a maturity scale, so 'are we ready' becomes a defensible score, not an opinion. From that I produce the package: a current-state report, a use-case inventory, a prioritization matrix, a risk register, a 90-day pilot, a three-year roadmap with budget, and an executive presentation. The whole thing ends with a clear, prioritized recommendation the leadership team can act on."

**How to answer:** Structure it as discover, assess, recommend. Name the readiness dimensions to show you have a framework, and end with the concrete deliverable package. Emphasize "score, not opinion."

**Why they ask this:** They want to know you have a repeatable method, not that you improvise. A named framework and a defined deliverable set signal a professional who has done this before.

## Q2. How do you present the same AI project to a CEO versus a regulator?

**Strong answer:**
"Same facts, different frame, because they care about different things. To the CEO I lead with business value, strategic fit, ROI, and the decision I need from them - I keep the technical detail light and the story about competitive advantage and risk-adjusted return. To a regulator I lead with compliance, accountability, data protection, and how we control and audit the system - I foreground governance, the risk register, human oversight, and evidence that controls work, and I make no claims I cannot support. The underlying assessment is identical; what changes is what I open with, how much detail I show, and which risks I emphasize. Tailoring the message is not spin - it is respecting that each audience is accountable for something different."

**How to answer:** State "same facts, different frame." Give the CEO's priorities (value, ROI, decision) and the regulator's (compliance, accountability, evidence, oversight). Stress that the substance is constant and you never make unsupported claims to either.

**Why they ask this:** Executive communication is the skill that separates a consultant from an engineer. They are testing whether you can carry one truth to very different rooms without either dumbing it down or overselling it.

## Q3. How do you price a consulting engagement and control scope?

**Strong answer:**
"I match the pricing model to the risk and clarity of the work. Fixed-fee when scope is well defined and I can estimate it confidently. Time-and-materials when the work is exploratory. Value-based when the outcome is measurable and large. A retainer for ongoing advisory. Then I control scope through the statement of work: an explicit in-scope list and, just as important, an explicit out-of-scope list, deliverables tied to acceptance criteria, milestones, and a change-request process. When new asks come up - and they always do - I route every one, even small ones, through a change request that shows the timeline and fee impact, so the client decides with eyes open. Scope control is not saying no to everything; it is making the cost of yes visible and protecting the deliverable I promised."

**How to answer:** Name the four pricing models and when each fits. Then make the SOW and change-request process the scope-control mechanism. Emphasize the explicit out-of-scope list and routing every ask through change requests.

**Why they ask this:** This is the commercial core of consulting. They want to know you can make money on the work and not bleed it away to scope creep - a very common way engagements fail.

## Q4. How do you handle employee resistance to an AI rollout?

**Strong answer:**
"Resistance is almost always a people problem, not a technology problem, so I diagnose the cause before pushing. Using a model like ADKAR, I check whether people know WHY it is happening, whether they WANT to, and whether they CAN. The usual blockers are fear for their jobs, being handed the tool without any say, distrust of accountability, no time or training, and incentives that reward the old behaviour. I address each: I get leadership to be honest about job impact - dodging it destroys trust - I involve respected staff in the design and recruit champions, I provide real training and protected time, I make clear the human always reviews and owns the output, and I align supervisors' measurement so using the tool is rewarded, not punished. A mandate alone produces malicious compliance, so I talk sponsors out of that and fix the actual blocker instead."

**How to answer:** Lead with "people problem, not technology problem" and a diagnostic model (ADKAR). Name the causes, then a specific remedy for each. Explicitly reject the bare mandate.

**Why they ask this:** Most AI projects that fail, fail at adoption, not at the model. They want proof you can land the change with humans, which is where the value is actually captured.

## Q5. Walk me through your consulting deliverables. What do we actually get?

**Strong answer:**
"A complete, decision-ready package. An executive summary for leadership. A current-state assessment and a readiness score so you know where you stand. A use-case inventory and a prioritization matrix so you know what to do first. A risk register so you know what could go wrong and who owns each risk. A target architecture and a governance model so you know how it will be built and controlled. A 90-day pilot charter with success metrics, a three-year roadmap, and a budget so you have a plan and a number. And a final presentation that ties it together for the room. Every deliverable has a clear audience and a clear decision it supports - I do not produce documents for their own sake."

**How to answer:** Group them by the question each answers: where are we (current-state, readiness), what do we do (inventory, matrix, roadmap), what could go wrong (risk register, governance), and how do we start (pilot, budget). End on "every deliverable supports a decision."

**Why they ask this:** They want to know exactly what they are buying and that it is actionable, not a shelf-ware report. Framing each deliverable by the decision it drives shows you deliver outcomes.

## Q6. A client wants to keep adding work without paying more. What do you do?

**Strong answer:**
"I handle it without souring the relationship. First I separate genuine clarification of work already in scope, which I just absorb, from new work outside the SOW, which I route through a change request. The change request is not a bill - it is a one-page decision aid that shows what the ask adds, its timeline and fee impact, and options: approve as a paid add-on, defer to a follow-on, drop it, or trade it for something in scope. I frame it as protecting their outcome - on-time, high-quality delivery of what they hired me for - not as guarding my margin. The key discipline is routing every out-of-scope ask through the process, even small ones, because it is the small exceptions that quietly blow the budget and the deadline."

**How to answer:** Distinguish in-scope clarification (absorb) from out-of-scope work (change request). Present the change request as a decision aid with options, framed as protecting the client's outcome. Stress "even small ones."

**Why they ask this:** Scope creep is the number-one margin killer. They want to see you can hold the line commercially while staying a partner, not a gatekeeper.

## Q7. How do you build trust with a skeptical technical stakeholder like a CISO?

**Strong answer:**
"I earn it with specifics, not confidence. I drop marketing phrases like 'secure by design' and instead map the real threats to concrete controls - for an LLM system I ground it in the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications: prompt injection, sensitive-information disclosure, excessive agency, supply-chain and data-residency risk - and for each I name a specific control and how we would prove it works. I answer their concrete questions directly, including where their data goes and under what contractual terms. And I am honest about residual risk and how we monitor it, because claiming nothing can go wrong is exactly what makes a good CISO distrust you. Then I invite them to co-own the security acceptance criteria, which turns them from a blocker into a partner."

**How to answer:** Replace slogans with a threat-to-control map on a shared framework (OWASP LLM Top 10). Answer specifics directly, concede residual risk, and make the CISO a co-owner. Honesty is the trust move.

**Why they ask this:** Technical stakeholders can kill an engagement. They want to see you can hold your ground with evidence and turn a skeptic into an ally rather than losing the room.

---

## Q8. How do you make sure the client is self-sufficient after you leave?

**Strong answer:**
"Handover is a deliverable, not an afterthought. I build the governance model, runbooks, and documentation so the client can operate and control what we built without me. I train their people rather than doing everything myself, and I recruit and equip internal champions who carry it forward. The roadmap I leave is theirs to execute, with a clear operating model and success metrics they own. My test is the same one I hold for any project: could the client's own team run and extend this using only what I documented and taught? If they still depend on me for basic operation, I have not finished the job. Recurring support, if they want it, is a separate, optional retainer - not a dependency I engineered in."

**How to answer:** Frame handover as a deliverable: documentation, training, champions, an owned operating model. End on the self-sufficiency test. Note that ongoing support is optional, not an engineered dependency.

**Why they ask this:** Clients fear consultants who make themselves indispensable. They want proof you leave them stronger and independent, which is also what earns referrals.

---

## Practice tips

- Structure the big-process answers (readiness, deliverables) with a clear skeleton so you never ramble - discover/assess/recommend, or group deliverables by the decision each supports.
- For communication questions, always say "same facts, different frame" and name what each audience cares about.
- For commercial questions, make the SOW and the change-request process your scope-control mechanism, and know the four pricing models cold.
- For people questions, lead with "people problem, not technology problem" and name a change model (ADKAR or Kotter).
- Honesty is a recurring trust move - about residual risk, about job impact, about a case that does not clear the hurdle rate. Use it deliberately.
- Keep answers to 60-90 seconds. Answer the actual question, then stop.
