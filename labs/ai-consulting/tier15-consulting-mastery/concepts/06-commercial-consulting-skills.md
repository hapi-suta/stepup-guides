# Concepts: Commercial Consulting Skills

**Tier 15, Module 15.6** - The business and legal craft that turns your AI expertise into paid, protected, repeatable engagements.

Technical skill gets you in the room. Commercial skill keeps you in business. As an AI consultant you are running a professional-services firm, even if that firm is just you. Every engagement is a small commercial transaction with a scope, a price, a set of promises, and a set of risks. If you cannot package a service, write a proposal, defend a scope, price the work sensibly, and protect yourself with sound contract terms, you will do great work and still lose money, get sued, or burn out. This module covers the deliverables of the commercial side: the documents and frameworks that make consulting a durable business rather than a series of favors.

Note: the templates and clause language below are teaching examples, not legal advice. Always have a qualified attorney review your standard contracts before you use them with real clients.

---

## Service packaging

A "service" a client can buy is not the same as a "thing you can do." Clients buy outcomes with a clear name, boundary, and price, not open-ended access to your brain. Packaging is the act of turning your capability into named, describable offers.

### Why packaging matters

- It makes you easy to buy. A buyer can say yes to "AI Readiness Assessment, 3 weeks, [PRICE]" far faster than to "some consulting."
- It sets expectations before money changes hands.
- It lets you reuse deliverable templates, so margin improves each time you sell the same package.

### A simple package structure

Every package should answer five questions in one page:

```
PACKAGE NAME:      AI Opportunity Assessment
WHO IT IS FOR:     Mid-size firms exploring their first AI use case
THE PROBLEM:       "We know AI matters but do not know where to start"
WHAT YOU GET:      - Prioritized backlog of 5-8 AI use cases
                   - Feasibility + ROI score per use case
                   - 90-day roadmap for the top recommendation
DURATION / PRICE:  3 weeks / [PRICE]
```

### A productized ladder

Offer a small entry point, a core engagement, and an ongoing tier. This lets clients start small and grow with you.

| Tier | Example package | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | Half-day AI strategy workshop | Low-risk first purchase, builds trust |
| Core | Use-case build or assessment | The main revenue engagement |
| Ongoing | Monthly advisory retainer | Recurring revenue, deepens relationship |

## Proposals

A proposal is a persuasion document, not a legal document. Its job is to get a yes. It restates the client's problem in their own words, presents your recommended approach, and makes the decision easy. The Statement of Work (next section) is where the binding detail lives.

### Anatomy of a strong proposal

1. Executive summary - the problem and your recommendation in five sentences a busy executive can read.
2. Situation and objectives - prove you listened. Use [CLIENT]'s own language.
3. Proposed approach - phases, not a task dump.
4. Deliverables - concrete, nameable artifacts.
5. Investment - the price, framed as investment against the value at stake.
6. Timeline - weeks or milestones, not exact dates you cannot control.
7. Why us - short, specific proof you have done this before.
8. Next steps - one clear action, for example "sign and return the SOW to reserve a start date."

### Proposal rules of thumb

- Lead with the client's outcome, not your methodology.
- Offer options (good / better / best) so the decision shifts from "yes or no" to "which one." In practice this tends to raise average deal size and gives the client a sense of control; treat the uplift as a well-supported pricing tactic, not a guarantee. (see: https://hbr.org/2018/09/the-good-better-best-approach-to-pricing)
- Put the price after the value, never on page one alone.
- Keep it short. A 4 to 6 page proposal that gets read beats a 30 page one that does not.

## Statements of work (SOW)

The SOW is the contract for a specific engagement. Where the proposal sells, the SOW governs. If a dispute arises, this is the document everyone reads. It should be boring, precise, and complete.

### SOW skeleton

```
STATEMENT OF WORK
Between: [CONSULTANT/COMPANY] ("Consultant") and [CLIENT] ("Client")
Effective date: [DATE]   |   Governing agreement: MSA dated [DATE]

1. BACKGROUND / OBJECTIVES
   Brief statement of what the Client wants and why.

2. SCOPE OF SERVICES (IN SCOPE)
   Numbered, specific tasks the Consultant will perform.

3. OUT OF SCOPE
   Explicitly what is NOT included. This section prevents disputes.

4. DELIVERABLES
   Each deliverable named, described, and tied to a milestone.

5. MILESTONES & SCHEDULE
   What is delivered when. Note dependencies on Client.

6. CLIENT RESPONSIBILITIES / ASSUMPTIONS
   Access, data, decisions, SMEs, environments the Client must provide.

7. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA
   How each deliverable is judged complete (see Acceptance section).

8. FEES & PAYMENT
   Pricing model, amounts, invoicing schedule, payment terms (e.g. Net 15).

9. CHANGE CONTROL
   How scope changes are requested, priced, and approved.

10. TERM & TERMINATION
    Duration, notice period, what happens to work + fees on termination.

11. SIGNATURES
```

### The two most valuable sections

- Out of scope. Most engagement pain comes from what the client assumed was included. Name the boundaries explicitly.
- Client responsibilities and assumptions. AI work is blocked constantly by missing data access, absent decision-makers, or dirty data. If you promised a model by week 4 but did not get data until week 3 because the client stalled, the assumptions section is what protects your timeline and your fee.

## Scope control

Scope creep is the slow, unbilled expansion of work through small "quick favors." It is the number one killer of consulting profitability. Scope control is not being difficult, it is being professional.

### How creep happens

- "While you are in there, could you also..."
- "This is basically the same thing we agreed, right?"
- A stakeholder who was not in the kickoff adds new requirements.
- The problem turns out bigger than either party understood.

### How to control it

1. Write a tight SOW with an explicit out-of-scope list.
2. Name creep the moment you see it, warmly: "Happy to do that. It is outside our current SOW, so let me write a quick change request with the effort and cost."
3. Never do meaningful extra work for free. Even if you decide to absorb something small, log it as a no-charge change so the client sees the value you gave.
4. Route all new requests through the change-control process (see below).

The phrase to memorize: "That is a great idea. It is a change to our scope, so here is what it would take." This keeps the relationship positive while protecting the boundary.

## Pricing

How you price shapes your incentives, your risk, and your income ceiling. There is no single right model. Choose per engagement based on how well-defined the work is and where the risk sits.

### The four core models

| Model | How it works | When to use | Pros | Cons | Who carries the risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Time and materials (T&M) | Bill by hour or day at a set rate | Discovery, unclear scope, evolving research, staff-augmentation | Simple, low risk to you, flexes with change | Income capped by your hours, client fears open-ended cost, penalizes your efficiency | Client (pays for all time) |
| Fixed-fee | One agreed price for a defined deliverable | Well-scoped, repeatable work (assessments, defined builds) | Predictable for both sides, rewards your efficiency, easy to sell | You eat overruns, requires tight scope + strong change control | Consultant (eats overruns) |
| Value-based | Price tied to the business value created, not effort | Clear, large, measurable client value (e.g. cost saved, revenue enabled) | Highest earning ceiling, aligns you with client outcomes | Hard to quantify value, needs trust + attribution, longer sales cycle | Shared (both bet on outcome) |
| Retainer | Fixed recurring fee for ongoing access or a monthly scope | Ongoing advisory, support, fractional roles | Predictable recurring revenue, deep relationship | Can drift to unlimited demands, must define included scope | Consultant (if scope undefined) |

### Practical pricing guidance

- Use T&M when you genuinely cannot scope the work yet. A short paid T&M discovery often precedes a fixed-fee build.
- Move to fixed-fee as soon as the work is knowable. Clients prefer certainty, and you keep the upside of working fast.
- Reach for value-based only when you can point to a specific, agreed number ("this saves [CLIENT] roughly [AMOUNT] per year"). Anchor your fee to a fraction of that value.
- For retainers, always state what is and is not included, and cap hours or set a fair-use clause so the retainer does not become unlimited on-call labor.
- Never price purely on your cost. Price on the value and the risk you are absorbing.

### Common trap

Charging fixed-fee on vague scope. You take on the client's uncertainty at your own expense. If scope is fuzzy, either scope it first (paid discovery) or price T&M.

## Milestones

Milestones break an engagement into checkpoints that carry meaning: a deliverable is handed over, a decision is made, and often a payment is triggered. They protect your cash flow and give the client visible progress.

### Good milestone design

- Tie payment to milestones, not the calendar. "50% on signing, 25% at design approval, 25% at final acceptance" beats "monthly" because each payment maps to delivered value.
- Make each milestone a client-verifiable event, not an internal task.
- Front-load some payment. A deposit on signing weeds out non-serious clients and funds your early work.

Example milestone schedule:

```
M1  Kickoff + data access confirmed        -> 30% invoice
M2  Use-case backlog + feasibility scored  -> 30% invoice
M3  Roadmap delivered + accepted           -> 40% invoice
```

## Acceptance criteria

Acceptance criteria define, in advance, what "done" means for each deliverable. Without them, "done" becomes whatever the client feels like on a given day, and you can be trapped in endless unpaid revisions.

### Writing good acceptance criteria

- Make them objective and testable. "The model achieves at least 85% precision on the agreed holdout set" beats "the model works well."
- Define an acceptance window: the client has, say, 5 business days to review and either accept or give specific written reasons for rejection.
- Add deemed acceptance: if the client does not respond within the window, the deliverable is deemed accepted. This stops silent stalling from blocking your payment.
- Limit revision rounds. "Includes up to two rounds of revisions; further changes handled via change request."

Example clause:

```
ACCEPTANCE. Client will review each Deliverable within five (5) business
days of receipt. Client will either accept it or provide a written list of
specific deficiencies against the Acceptance Criteria in Section 7. If Client
does not respond within the review period, the Deliverable is deemed accepted.
Consultant will correct accepted deficiencies within [N] business days.
```

## Change requests

A change request (CR) is the formal, lightweight process for handling anything outside the current SOW. It is how scope control actually happens in practice. It does not need to be heavy - one page or even one email is fine, as long as it captures scope, effort, cost, and sign-off.

### Change request template

```
CHANGE REQUEST #[N]
Engagement: [SOW reference]           Date: [DATE]
Requested by: [NAME, CLIENT]

WHAT IS CHANGING:
   Plain description of the new or changed work.

IMPACT ON SCOPE:
   What is added, removed, or altered.

IMPACT ON SCHEDULE:
   New or shifted dates / milestones.

IMPACT ON FEES:
   Additional cost, or no charge, and the pricing basis.

APPROVAL:
   Client signature ____________   Consultant signature ____________
```

### The discipline

- No approved CR, no changed work. This one rule prevents most disputes.
- Even zero-cost changes get a CR so the client sees goodwill and the record stays clean.
- Keep CRs numbered and attached to the SOW.

## Intellectual property (IP)

IP terms decide who owns what you create. This matters a lot in AI work, where you may reuse frameworks, prompts, code libraries, and models across many clients. Get this wrong and you either give away your reusable toolkit or you accidentally promise a client something you already licensed to someone else.

### The key distinction

- Background IP - what you bring in. Your pre-existing templates, libraries, prompt patterns, methodologies. You should retain ownership and grant the client a license to use it within the deliverable.
- Foreground IP - what you create specifically for this client. The custom deliverable itself. Clients usually expect to own this, often on full payment.

### Practical approach

- Retain your background IP. Grant the client a non-exclusive, perpetual license to use it as embedded in their deliverable.
- Assign foreground IP to the client, but make assignment conditional on full payment. Until paid, you keep it.
- Keep a general improvements clause so you can reuse the generic know-how and skills you gain - just not the client's confidential specifics.

Example clause:

```
IP OWNERSHIP. Consultant retains all rights to Background IP (pre-existing
tools, templates, methods) and grants Client a non-exclusive, perpetual
license to use Background IP as incorporated in the Deliverables. Upon full
payment, Consultant assigns to Client all rights in the Foreground IP created
specifically for Client under this SOW. Nothing limits Consultant's right to
use general skills, know-how, and experience gained.
```

## Confidentiality

Clients share sensitive information - strategy, data, customer lists, internal problems. Confidentiality (NDA) terms govern how you handle it. In AI consulting this is heightened because you often touch the client's proprietary data directly.

### What good confidentiality terms cover

- Definition of Confidential Information (usually anything marked confidential or that a reasonable person would treat as such).
- Your obligation not to disclose or use it except to perform the work.
- Standard carve-outs: information that is public, already known to you, or independently developed.
- Duration: how long the obligation survives after the engagement (often 2 to 5 years, sometimes indefinite for trade secrets).
- Return or destruction of confidential materials on request or at engagement end.

### AI-specific caution

Do not paste a client's confidential data into third-party AI tools unless the contract permits it and the tool's terms protect the data. Free consumer AI tools may train on inputs. When in doubt, use enterprise or zero-retention offerings and disclose your tooling to the client.

## Data-processing terms

When you handle personal or regulated data on a client's behalf, you become a data processor and the client is the data controller. Under the EU/UK GDPR this controller-processor split is a defined legal relationship, and Article 28 requires the processing to be governed by a written contract, commonly called a data-processing agreement (DPA). This is separate from general confidentiality. (see: https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/)

### What a DPA typically specifies

- The purpose and scope of processing (only what the engagement needs).
- That you process personal data only on documented instructions from the client.
- Security measures you will maintain.
- Rules on sub-processors (you cannot bring in a subcontractor who touches the data without approval).
- Breach notification obligations and timelines.
- Data return or deletion at the end of the engagement.
- Any cross-border data transfer safeguards.

### Practical guidance

- If your AI work touches personal data (customer records, health, financial, biometric), assume you need a DPA and raise it early.
- Minimize: only take the data you truly need, and prefer anonymized or synthetic data where possible.
- Be explicit about where data lives and which AI services process it.

## Professional liability

Liability terms cap and shape your exposure if something goes wrong. AI deliverables can fail in costly ways - a model makes bad predictions, an automation causes an error at scale. You cannot eliminate risk, but you can bound it.

### Core protections

- Limitation of liability - cap your total liability, commonly at the fees paid under the SOW. This is standard and reasonable in professional services.
- Exclusion of indirect damages - exclude consequential, incidental, and lost-profit damages so a small fee cannot expose you to a huge downstream claim.
- Disclaimer on AI outputs - state that AI/ML outputs are probabilistic, that the client is responsible for human review and final decisions, and that you do not guarantee specific outcomes.
- Professional liability insurance (errors and omissions, E&O) - carry it. It is your backstop when a limitation clause is challenged.

Example clause:

```
LIMITATION OF LIABILITY. Consultant's total liability under this SOW will not
exceed the total fees paid by Client under it. Neither party is liable for
indirect, incidental, or consequential damages, including lost profits. Client
acknowledges that AI/ML outputs are probabilistic and remains responsible for
reviewing and validating all outputs before relying on them.
```

## Subcontractors

As you grow you may bring in other specialists. Subcontractors extend your capacity but create risk: they see client data, produce deliverables the client relies on, and can leak your IP or their own conflicts into the engagement.

### Managing subcontractors safely

- Get client permission if the SOW or DPA requires approval for sub-processors, especially where data is involved.
- Flow down your obligations. Your subcontractor agreement must bind them to the same confidentiality, IP assignment, and data terms you promised the client. Otherwise you have a gap.
- Assign their IP to you first, so you can in turn assign it to the client cleanly.
- You remain responsible to the client. From the client's view, the subcontractor's mistakes are yours. Price and manage accordingly.

## Recurring support

Many engagements should not end at delivery. AI systems drift, data changes, and clients need help operating what you built. Recurring support turns one-off projects into stable recurring revenue and deepens the relationship.

### Common recurring models

| Model | What the client gets | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Support retainer | Guaranteed response + set hours per month | Post-launch maintenance |
| Advisory retainer | Regular strategic access, review calls | Ongoing guidance without a build |
| Managed service | You operate/monitor the system for them | Clients without in-house capability |
| Fractional role | Part-time embedded AI leadership | Firms not ready for a full-time hire |

### Design rules

- Define included scope and boundaries. "Up to [N] hours per month; additional work via change request."
- Set service levels you can actually meet (for example "response within one business day"), not aspirational ones.
- Bill in advance and auto-renew with a notice period, so revenue is predictable.
- Review the retainer periodically. If the client consistently uses far more or far less than agreed, adjust the tier rather than quietly absorbing the overage.

The best time to sell recurring support is at the end of a successful project, when trust is highest and the client is worried about "what happens now."

## Check yourself (Exit gate for this concept)

You are ready to move on when you can:

1. Take a vague client request and produce a one-page proposal plus a Statement of Work with in-scope, out-of-scope, deliverables, milestones, acceptance criteria, and a change-control clause.
2. Choose and justify a pricing model (T&M, fixed-fee, value-based, or retainer) for a given engagement, explaining who carries the risk and why that model fits.
3. Identify the contract terms that protect you - IP split (background vs foreground), confidentiality, data-processing, and limitation of liability - and explain in plain language what each one does.
