# SURVIVE Runbook: Scope Creep Mid-Engagement

**Tier 15 - SURVIVE scenario 1 of 3**

This is a review-assessed scenario. There is no script to run. You control an expanding engagement using change requests and a reviewer judges whether you protected both the deliverable and the relationship.

---

## The situation

You are mid-way through a fixed-fee AI-readiness assessment for [CLIENT]. The signed statement of work covers discovery, a current-state report, a use-case inventory, a prioritization model, a risk register, a 90-day pilot design, a three-year roadmap, and an executive presentation.

Over three weeks, extra asks have crept in, one friendly email at a time:
- "While you're in here, can you also review our existing chatbot vendor contract?"
- "Can you add a full data-governance policy? Legal would love that."
- "Can you build a working prototype of the top use case so the board can see it?"
- "Can you brief our regional managers too, not just the exec team?"

None of these are in the SOW. Each was framed as small. Together they would add weeks of unpaid work, blow the timeline, and dilute the assessment you were actually hired to deliver. Your project manager on the client side keeps saying "it's all part of AI, right?" If you keep saying yes, you lose money, miss the deadline, and train the client to expect unlimited scope. If you say no bluntly, you look rigid and unhelpful.

Your job: stop the bleed using change requests, without becoming the consultant who says no to everything.

---

## Why this happens

- **Fixed-fee work attracts scope creep** because the client's marginal cost of asking feels like zero.
- **Each ask is individually small,** so refusing any one feels petty. The damage is cumulative.
- **"It's all AI" blurs the boundary.** Adjacent-sounding work is not in-scope work.
- **No one is tracking scope against the SOW,** so drift is invisible until the deadline is at risk.
- **Saying yes feels like good service** in the moment and is the single most common way engagements lose money and miss deadlines.

Scope control is not obstruction. It is how you deliver what you promised, on time, at the price you quoted. The tool for it is the change request, which you wrote into the SOW for exactly this.

---

## Diagnosis: sort the asks against the SOW

Do not react ask by ask. Lay them all against the signed scope and classify each:

| Ask | In SOW? | Classification | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Review chatbot vendor contract | No | Out of scope, adjacent | Change request |
| Full data-governance policy | No | Out of scope, large | Change request (or later engagement) |
| Build a working prototype | No | Out of scope, large + risky | Change request (or later engagement) |
| Brief regional managers | No | Out of scope, small | Change request or goodwill trade |
| Clarify a use case already in scope | Yes | In scope | Just do it |

Two categories matter:
- **Genuine clarification of in-scope work:** absorb it, do not bill it, do not make a fuss.
- **New work outside the SOW:** route every one through the change-request process. No exceptions, however small, because the exceptions are what kill you.

---

## Recovery: control scope with change requests

### 1. Make the drift visible, without blame
Send a short, friendly note: "A few new requests have come up beyond our signed scope. I want to make sure they get handled properly rather than quietly slipping the timeline, so here's how I'll track them." You are framing scope control as protecting THEIR outcome (on-time delivery), not defending your margin.

### 2. Reaffirm the current SOW and its deadline
Restate what is in scope and the delivery date. This re-anchors everyone to what "done" means and shows the new asks are additions, not omissions on your part.

### 3. Log each new ask as a change request
For each out-of-scope item, produce a one-page change request:

```
Change Request CR-00X
- Requested by:      [name / date]
- Description:       what the client asked for
- Impact on scope:   what it adds
- Impact on timeline: +X days / weeks
- Impact on fee:      +$ (or "trade against Y")
- Options:           (a) approve now  (b) defer to a follow-on
                     (c) drop         (d) swap for an in-scope item
- Decision needed by: [date, to protect the deadline]
```

The change request is not a bill. It is a decision aid that makes the cost of "yes" visible so the client chooses with eyes open.

### 4. Offer options, not just a price
For each: approve as paid add-on, defer to a follow-on engagement, drop it, or trade it for something in scope (e.g. "I can brief regional managers instead of running the second exec workshop, at no extra cost - your call"). Options make you a partner, not a gatekeeper.

### 5. Protect the core deliverable explicitly
Say plainly: "If we absorb all of these, the assessment you hired me for will be late and thinner. I would rather deliver that excellently and handle the extras as a clean follow-on." This reframes your no as protecting quality of the thing they care about most.

### 6. Get a decision, then move on
Route the change requests to whoever controls budget and scope on the client side (usually the sponsor, not the person who asked). Once decided, update the SOW or the follow-on backlog, and continue. Do not let items sit in limbo.

---

## What you must produce for this scenario

1. **A classified list of all asks** against the signed SOW (in-scope clarification vs out-of-scope new work).
2. **A change request** (one page, using the format above) for each out-of-scope item, with timeline and fee impact and at least three options.
3. **A short note to the sponsor** that reaffirms current scope and deadline, presents the change requests, and protects the core deliverable.
4. **An updated scope position**: what stays in this engagement, what moves to a follow-on, what is dropped or traded.

## Decision checklist (self-assess or reviewer-assess)

- [ ] You classified every ask against the signed SOW, not by gut feel.
- [ ] Genuine in-scope clarifications were absorbed without fuss.
- [ ] Every out-of-scope ask, even small ones, went through a change request - no silent exceptions.
- [ ] Each change request shows explicit timeline AND fee impact.
- [ ] You offered options (approve / defer / drop / trade), not just a "no" or a bill.
- [ ] You routed decisions to whoever actually controls scope and budget.
- [ ] You explicitly protected the core deliverable's quality and deadline.
- [ ] The relationship reads as partner-protecting-outcome, not gatekeeper-guarding-margin.

If any box is unchecked, you are still leaking scope. Rework before you send anything.

---

## What you learned

- Scope creep kills fixed-fee engagements one small yes at a time. The damage is cumulative and invisible until the deadline.
- The change request is your control mechanism - it makes the cost of "yes" visible so the client decides with eyes open.
- Route every out-of-scope ask through it, including small ones. The exceptions are what sink you.
- Frame scope control as protecting the client's outcome (on-time, high-quality delivery), and always offer options, so you are a partner, not a gatekeeper.
